# Argument craft

Formatting is solved elsewhere in this skill. This file is about whether the
document is any good. Read it before drafting the argument section of anything.

---

## 1. Selection: the hardest decision is what to leave out

A brief is judged by its weakest argument, not its strongest. A court that reads
three good points and two bad ones does not average them; it discounts the writer.

**Rank every candidate argument on two axes: how likely it is to win, and how much
it costs if it loses.** Then:

- **Lead with the argument that is both strong and narrow.** Give the court the
  easiest path to the result you want. Judges take easy paths.
- **Prefer a small number of developed arguments when the issues permit.** Do not
  impose an arbitrary count or omit a necessary jurisdictional, alternative, or
  preservation argument to meet a style target.
- **Identify dependencies between arguments.** State an alternative expressly
  when the court would reach it only after rejecting the primary position.
- **Cut anything you would be embarrassed to defend at a hearing.** If you would not
  say it out loud to the judge, it does not belong in writing either.
- **Give preservation arguments the development the law requires.** Verify what
  must be raised and supported; one sentence is not automatically enough to
  preserve an issue. Recommend cuts only after checking their procedural cost.

The Doe sever motion (`worked-example.md`) makes two arguments and orders them so the second is
unnecessary if the first succeeds. That is the shape to aim for.

## 2. Ordering: strongest first, unless a threshold issue comes first

Default order is descending strength. Two exceptions:

- **Threshold and jurisdictional questions come first** regardless of strength,
  because the court has to reach them first.
- **Where argument B is only reachable if the court rejects argument A, A comes
  first**, and B opens with "Even if..." That framing tells the court exactly what
  work each argument is doing.

Then close with the argument that makes the others cheaper. The Doe motion ends with
"because the counts are misjoined, prejudice is presumed", a point that, if the
court accepts the first argument, means it never has to weigh prejudice at all.
Putting it last rather than first keeps it as the payoff instead of the premise.

## 3. Structure inside a section: rule, sub-rule, application, answer

Every argument section does four things in this order.

1. **The rule, with its citation.** One or two sentences.
2. **The sub-rule or factor list**, quoted, if the law supplies one.
3. **The application** to these facts, naming the record. This is where the work is
   and it should be the longest part.
4. **The answer to the best counter-argument.**

The most common failure is spending four paragraphs on step 1 and one sentence on
step 3. A court knows the law. It does not know your record. Invert the ratio.

**Walk factor lists, do not assert them.** If a case supplies seven factors, take
them in order and say what each one does here, even if some take one sentence. A
brief that quotes a seven-factor test and then declares "every factor cuts against
joinder" without walking them has not made the argument. One that walks them has.

## 4. Answering the other side before they speak

Name the opponent's best response and answer it in the body, not in a footnote:

> Counsel anticipates the State may respond that the June 1 arrest occurred while
> officers were attempting to locate Mr. Doe on warrants issued during the
> domestic-violence investigation.

(That is the attorney form. Signing pro se, write "Plaintiff anticipates that
[opponent] will respond that…". "Counsel anticipates" over a pro se signature reads
as a phrase copied from a form book.)

Then answer it on two levels: it is not in the record, **and** even if it were, it
would not get them where they need to go. That two-level answer is the pattern
worth copying. It denies the fact and denies its significance, so conceding the
first does not cost you the second.

**Do not do this for every possible response.** One or two, the ones a competent
opponent will actually make. A brief that pre-empts six arguments looks anxious.

## 5. Concede early and narrowly

Conceding what you must, in your own words and before the other side makes you,
buys credibility that carries into the contested parts:

> Mr. Doe recognizes that relief requires a showing of "substantial prejudice."

That sentence costs nothing, because the argument was going to have to meet that
standard anyway. What it buys is a court that believes the writer's other
characterizations of the law.

**What to concede:** the standard, unfavorable controlling authority, a fact that is
plainly true. **What not to concede:** anything the other side would have to prove
and has not.

## 6. Bad facts

Never hide a bad fact. Opposing counsel will use it and the court will notice you
did not mention it, which is worse than the fact.

- **Surface it yourself**, in the fact section, in neutral language, positioned
  where it does the least damage: after the context that explains it, not as the
  opening line of a paragraph.
- **Then deal with it in the argument**, on the merits.
- **Do not characterize it away.** "Plaintiff arguably did not..." reads as an
  admission that he did.

The same goes for adverse authority. Cite it, distinguish it, and if it cannot be
distinguished, say what makes this case different in a way that does not require the
court to overrule anything.

## 7. Sentences and paragraphs

- **One idea per paragraph, and the idea goes in the first sentence.** A judge
  skimming should be able to read only first sentences and follow the argument.
- **Short sentences for the points that matter.** "What Counts 1-4 share with Counts
  5-13 is Mr. Doe's name, and nothing more." That sentence lands because the
  three around it are long.
- **Prefer the concrete.** "The complaint identifies no witness and no exhibit
  common to the two groups of counts" beats "there is insufficient evidentiary
  overlap."
- **Cut intensifiers.** "Clearly," "plainly," "obviously," and "it is beyond dispute
  that" all signal that the writer is worried. If it is clear, the reader will see
  it. If it is not, saying "clearly" will not help.
- **No rhetorical questions.** Make the statement.
- **Do not restate the standard of review** in every section. Once.

## 8. Length is a discipline, not a permission

**Neither La Crosse nor Trempealeau County limits brief length.** That is a trap.
The absence of a cap is not an invitation; it means nothing external will stop you
from writing a brief no one finishes.

Trempealeau Rule 6.VI decides summary judgment **without oral argument unless the
court directs otherwise**. In those cases the brief is not a preview of your
argument, it is the entire argument. That is a reason to make it good, not a reason
to make it long.

Working targets, absent a scheduling order that says otherwise: a non-dispositive
motion brief under 10 pages; a summary judgment brief under 25. If a draft runs
past those, the problem is almost always argument selection, not compression.

## 9. Writing pro se against represented parties

This is the posture this skill assumes by default, so it is worth naming directly. Courts do not
formally discount pro se filings, but readers form impressions fast, and pro se
filings have characteristic failure modes. Avoiding them is most of the battle.

**The failure modes, in rough order of how much damage they do:**

1. **Procedural grievance mixed into the merits.** How the other side has behaved,
   how the clerk treated you, what happened at an earlier hearing, none of it
   belongs in a brief about whether a motion should be granted. If misconduct
   matters, it gets its own motion with its own relief. Mixing it in tells the court
   the merits argument could not stand alone.
2. **Emotional register.** Indignation reads as weakness. State what happened and
   what rule it violates. The facts carry the weight, and they carry it better
   without adjectives.
3. **Everything raised at once.** A filing with nine arguments does not look
   thorough, it looks undisciplined. See § 1.
4. **Length without a cap.** See § 8.
5. **Citing the wrong body of law.** Federal cases for Wisconsin procedure, or
   out-of-state authority where Wisconsin has a case on point, marks the writer as
   working from a general search rather than the governing law.
6. **Quoting a statute or case without applying it.** A block quote is not an
   argument.
7. **Overclaiming.** One overstated characterization of a case, caught by opposing
   counsel, contaminates everything else you said.

**What actually builds credibility:**

- Getting the procedural mechanics exactly right. A correctly captioned motion with
  a proper notice of hearing, served on time, tells the court to read the substance.
- Narrow, specific relief the court can grant by signing something.
- Citing Wisconsin authority, current, and accurately.
- Conceding the standard you have to meet.
- Answering the best argument against you.
- Brevity.

**One thing to be honest with yourself about.** The strongest version of a filing is
usually the one that leaves out the part you most want to say. That instinct is not
a rule of law; it is a fact about readers.

## 10. Before you call a draft finished

Ask these five, honestly:

1. If the court read only my point headings, would it know what I want and why I
   should get it?
2. Can I say, in one sentence, what the strongest argument against me is, and does
   the brief answer it?
3. Is there an argument in here I would drop if I could only make two?
4. Does the relief I request in the conclusion match, word for word, the relief in
   the motion?
5. Is there anything in here I put in because it felt good to write?

Question 5 catches more bad filings than the other four combined.
