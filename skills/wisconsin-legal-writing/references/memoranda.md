# Memoranda: the objective memo to a supervising attorney

This file covers the internal research memorandum: a nonlawyer or junior writer
answering a legal question for a supervising attorney. It is not a filing.
Nothing here goes to a court, the builder does not build it, and the caption
rules do not apply. What does apply, with full force, is Rule zero in SKILL.md:
never invent a citation, never invent a record fact, and mark what you could not
verify. A memo exists so that a lawyer can make a decision. A wrong memo
corrupts the decision, and the reader, by definition, has not done the research
that would catch it.

Some statutory quotations in this file originated from secondary reproductions
rather than the official Legislature text. Wisconsin court opinions and rules
published by wicourts.gov are primary court sources; do not label them unofficial
merely because that site is not the Legislature's statutory publisher.
Before any quotation from this file goes into a delivered memo, read the current
official text. That instruction is also the first thing the conventions block in
§ 3 makes the memo itself declare.

---

## 1. What a memo is for, and the one failure that matters

An objective memo predicts. A brief persuades. Georgetown Law's memo-to-brief
comparison states the split: a memo exists to "answer a legal question" and
"objectively inform the reader about what the law is"; a brief exists to
"inform AND persuade." Because "memos form the basis for legal decisions, be
honest about the strengths and weaknesses of your client's case."

The central discipline is refusing to slide from the first genre into the
second. The memo that argues instead of predicts is the characteristic failure
of the form, and it fails silently: it reads well, it feels strong, and it gives
the attorney a false read on whether to file, settle, or decline. Bloomberg
Law's framing is the right one: "a legal memo isn't the place to offer a legal
opinion. It should maintain an impartial tone, with no implied preference for
one side or the other."

The symptoms, in the order they usually appear in a draft:

1. The Brief Answer says "Yes" where the honest answer is "probably yes."
2. Adverse authority appears only in rebuttal posture ("Although *Smith*
   held..., that case is distinguishable") and never on its own terms.
3. Counter-analysis is a single sentence ending "but that argument fails."
4. The Facts section grows adjectives that presuppose the outcome.
5. Conditional language disappears: "the court will find" replaces "the court
   would likely find."
6. Nothing in the memo would surprise the person who assigned it.

Run the neutrality audit from Point First Writing on every draft: **would you
write the same analysis if you represented the other side?** If the answer is
no, the memo is a brief wearing a memo's headings.

Four priorities for an objective memo:

1. **State the weaknesses affirmatively.** Both memos and briefs must be accurate;
   advocacy does not authorize burying material adverse facts or controlling law.
   "You must include all facts, the good, the bad, and the ugly, even if
   it's not what your supervising attorney or client wants to hear" (NWTC).
2. **Cite adverse authority the other side has not found yet.** Bloomberg Law:
   "The omission of negative case law only compromises future legal strategy
   and heightens client legal exposure."
3. **Give a probability, not a demand.** The memo's output is "the court would
   probably hold X." The brief's output is "the court should hold X."
4. **Disclose research limits.** Identify a missing Wisconsin authority or an
   unresolved source question, as § 8 explains. A brief may also need to identify
   the absence of controlling authority; do not imply that contrary law does not
   exist merely because a search did not find it.

## 2. Architecture

The national six-part structure is Heading, Questions Presented, Brief Answers,
Facts, Discussion, Conclusion. The default form builds on it. In order:

```
Legend line (only if factually and legally supported): appropriate confidentiality/work-product label
MEMORANDUM (letterspaced, centered, double rule beneath)
To: / From: / Date: / Re:      <- Re is two full lines: matter, parties, what the memo decides
Conventions note block          <- smaller type, before the first heading; see § 3
I.   Questions Presented        <- numbered, each carrying its statutory or doctrinal hook
II.  Brief Answers              <- numbered to match, each opening with a bold calibrated verdict
III. Facts                      <- provenance stated in the first sentence
IV.  Discussion                 <- lettered subsections, full-sentence headings in bold italic
V.   Sensitivity Analysis       <- what would change the answer; see § 9
VI.  Conclusion and Recommendations
Closing note                    <- short, first person, candid
Respectfully submitted, / signature rule / name in bold / Legal Assistant
```

Continuation pages carry a running header; every page carries a `Page X of Y`
footer and repeats the legend in the footer.

**Which sections are load-bearing.** Questions Presented, Brief Answers, Facts,
and Discussion. The Questions and Brief Answers are what a busy supervisor
reads first; the Facts are what makes the prediction falsifiable; the
Discussion is what makes it checkable. The one section that must never be
dropped is the Facts: "All factual information that later appears in the
discussion section of the memorandum should be described in the facts section"
(CUNY). A Discussion that relies on a fact absent from the Facts is defective.

**Accepted variants.** Pairing each Brief Answer directly under its Question
Presented instead of running two parallel numbered lists; this is often better
in a multi-issue memo, and the reader never has to hold three questions in
memory. An introductory paragraph before the Questions Presented, which is the
Wisconsin paralegal-program convention (NWTC): heading, Re line, introductory
paragraph, then the questions. "Answers" as the section title instead of
"Brief Answers." Dropping the Conclusion in a short single-issue memo, where it
would only restate the Brief Answer. Placing the Facts before the Questions
Presented is seen in practice but is not an attested convention `[UNVERIFIED]`;
treat it as a default-style option and do not adopt it without being asked.

**The Re line is not a topic.** It carries the matter name, the parties, the
file or case number, and what the memo decides, and it is allowed to run two
full lines to do it. "Re: Example v. [X], La Crosse County Case No.
25-CV-____; whether the § ____ notice served March 3 was timely" is the form.
"Re: Notice question" is the failure. The Re line and the date are what tell a
reader eighteen months later whether the memo is still good and what it was
ever good for.

## 3. The conventions block

Before the first heading, in smaller type, the memo declares its own rules of
construction. This block is the best structural feature of the default form.
Keep it, and keep it honest: every sentence in it is a warranty, and a false
recital is worse than no block.

What it declares, and why each declaration earns its place:

1. **What quotation marks mean.** That quotation marks enclose only verbatim
   judicial, attorney general, statutory, or constitutional language
   transcribed from the source documents. This converts every quotation in the
   memo from an assertion into a checkable claim. It is the anti-fabrication
   rule of SKILL.md turned into a promise the reader can hold you to.
2. **How alterations are handled.** Initial capitalization and terminal
   punctuation conform to Bluebook R. 5.2; all other alterations are bracketed
   or elided. Without this line, the reader cannot tell a cleaned-up quotation
   from a doctored one.
3. **The statutory edition.** Which edition of the Wisconsin Statutes the memo
   cites, the Act through which it is updated, and the publication date.
   Statutes are amended and subsection numbering moves; the edition line is
   what lets a later reader decide whether the memo has gone stale.
4. **Renumbering.** Where decisions predate a renumbering, that they cite the
   old subsection, with the current one given. This is a Wisconsin-specific
   trap: a pre-renumbering case quoted accurately will cite a subsection that
   no longer says what the case says it says.
5. **A pointer to the closing verification note**, where quotation verification
   and pinpoint status are reported.
6. **The cite-check status.** That the memo was adversarially cite-checked
   against the source documents before circulation, with items still needing
   reporter or Lexis confirmation listed at the end. "Adversarially" means
   checked as an opponent would check it: open the source, confirm the language
   is at the pinpoint, confirm it says what it is cited for. If the check has
   not happened, the block must say so, not claim it.

Fill-in template:

```
Conventions. Quotation marks in this memorandum enclose only verbatim
judicial, statutory, or constitutional language transcribed from the source
documents. Initial capitalization and terminal punctuation conform to
Bluebook R. 5.2; all other alterations are bracketed or elided. Statutory
citations are to the [20__-__] Wisconsin Statutes, updated through 20__
Wis. Act ___ (published [date]). Decisions predating the renumbering of
§ ______ cite the former subsection; the current provision is § ______.
Quotation verification and pinpoint status are reported in the note at the
end of this memorandum. Verification performed: [identify actual sources,
checks, date, and coverage, or state NOT YET PERFORMED]. Outstanding items:
[identify unverified quotations, pinpoints, treatment checks, or other limits].
```

Adjust each bracket to what is actually true. If no renumbering is in play,
drop that sentence rather than leaving a hollow recital. Never certify that a
quotation, citation, treatment check, or adversarial review occurred unless it
actually did. Preserve unresolved items in the delivered memo's review status.

## 4. Questions Presented

The traditional formula is **under / does / when**: "Under [the legal rule],
does [the legal consequence follow] when [these facts exist]." By convention
it ends with a period, not a question mark. The three slots:

- **Under**: the governing legal source, with the jurisdiction named. "Under
  Wis. Stat. § 804.12(1)" or "Under Wisconsin law." A question that names no
  jurisdiction is a law-review question.
- **Does / Is / May**: the legal consequence at issue.
- **When**: the two to four legally significant facts that make this case's
  question rather than an abstract one.

Garner's multi-sentence "deep issue" form (separate sentences, premises first,
question last, under 75 words, ending with a question mark) is a legitimate
alternative for a genuinely complicated question. The one-sentence and
multi-sentence camps have not settled their dispute; do not pretend they have.
What every source agrees on: the question must name the jurisdiction and the
governing rule, must embed enough fact that the reader can tell which case
this is, and must not assume its own answer. "Under § 55.055(1)(b), is the
plainly defective consent invalid" has answered itself; save the conclusion
for the Brief Answer.

**How much fact.** A fact belongs in the Question Presented only if changing
it would change the Brief Answer. Everything else belongs in the Facts. (That
test is a synthesis, not a quoted rule. `[UNVERIFIED]` as an attributed
formulation, but use it; it works.)

**Multiple related questions.** Two rules. The questions must appear in the
same order the Discussion takes them, and a threshold question that could
dispose of the matter comes first. Where several questions share a governing
statute, state it fully in question 1 and use a short reference after: "Under
the same statute, ..." If three questions differ only in the verb, they are one
question with three sub-issues, and the sub-issues belong in the Discussion
headings, not in the Questions Presented.

## 5. Brief Answers and the calibration vocabulary

Each Brief Answer opens with a **bolded verdict**, then gives a self-contained
explanation of four or five sentences, generally without citations. If the
answer turns entirely on one statute or one case, naming it is acceptable.
Self-containment is the real test: the Brief Answer must be readable by
someone who reads nothing else, which means it restates the operative facts
compactly ("because he signed after the deadline") rather than referring back
("for the reasons stated in the Facts").

The verdict is calibrated, not a bare yes or no. The only calibration set
verifiably taught as a convention is the four-point **Yes / Probably yes /
Probably no / No**; everything beyond it is practitioner usage, not a codified
taxonomy `[UNVERIFIED]`. The default form goes further and uses record-scoped
verdicts: "Not on this record," "Yes as to the County, on the record as it
stands," "No on current facts," "Not realistically." Those are better than the
bare four-point set because they carry the condition inside the verdict. But a
calibrated vocabulary only works if the reader knows what each rung means, so
**define the ladder and then use it consistently**. The default ladder, in
descending confidence:

| Verdict | What it claims | Justified when |
|---|---|---|
| **Yes.** / **No.** | The answer is compelled. | Controlling statutory text or binding on-point precedent; no material factual dispute. Reserve the unhedged form for these and nothing else. |
| **Almost certainly yes/no.** | One small thing could upset it. | Binding precedent with a minor factual gap; name the gap. |
| **Probably yes/no.** | You would bet on it, and the counter-argument is real but weak. | Binding authority applied to analogous but not identical facts. This rung requires genuine counter-analysis in the Discussion. |
| **More likely than not.** | Contested, and this is the better side. | Genuinely disputed law or facts; you would still bet this way. |
| **The question is close.** | No responsible tilt. | Persuasive authority both ways, or a factor test with no clear lean. Must be paired with the sensitivity analysis in § 9, or it is a shrug. |
| **Not on this record.** | The answer could change, but the current facts do not get there. | The record as it stands fails a required element; say which one, and what evidence would supply it. |
| **Cannot be predicted on this record.** | A genuine dead end. | Reserved for real ones, and it must name what would resolve it. |

Three rules that make the ladder work:

1. **Attach the confidence to a reason, not just a modifier.** The useful form
   is verdict, then because, then what makes it less than certain: "Probably
   yes. The statute's text is unambiguous and the conduct falls within it. The
   uncertainty is factual, not legal: if the March 3 email is authenticated,
   the answer is close to certain; if it is not, the analysis never begins."
   Telling the reader what kind of uncertainty this is, legal or factual,
   resolvable or not, is worth more than any modifier.
2. **Scope the prediction.** "On this record," "on the facts as presently
   understood," "assuming the client's account is accurate." The phrase makes
   the prediction conditional on the fact base and points the reader at the
   sensitivity analysis.
3. **Never state a probability as a number.** "I estimate a 65% likelihood"
   claims a precision the method cannot support, and a supervising attorney
   will discount the whole memo for it.

"It depends" alone is the useless answer. It becomes useful the moment it is
converted into a conditional prediction: "If the tolling agreement is
enforceable, probably yes; if not, almost certainly no. Enforceability turns
on X, which I could not determine." That is a prediction, not a shrug.

And the matching-depth rule: the verdict word and the counter-analysis depth
must agree. A "probably yes" over a one-sided Discussion is a lie in either
the verdict or the analysis. A flat "No" on settled law does not need a
paragraph inventing an argument nobody will make.

## 6. Facts

### Selection

Three categories (NWTC): **legally significant facts**, which "directly lead
to or otherwise affect the legal outcome"; **contextual facts**, which provide
background without affecting outcomes; and **irrelevant facts**. Include the
first two, cut the third, and when in doubt include: "it's better to include
irrelevant facts than to exclude potentially legally significant facts."

Draft the Facts last. "You won't know what facts are relevant to your analysis
until you finish your research" (Point First Writing), so write the Discussion,
then write the Facts it needs, then confirm the two match: every fact the
Discussion uses appears in the Facts, and the Facts contain nothing the
Discussion never touches unless it is context.

Organize chronologically when the question turns on sequence, topically when
independent issues each have their own fact cluster, by party when multiple
actors have distinct roles. A chronological spine with flagged topical
digressions is normal.

### Unfavorable facts

The most repeated rule in the genre: all of them, always. "You must include
all facts, the good, the bad, and the ugly, even if it's not what your
supervising attorney or client wants to hear" (NWTC). "You do not do your
client a favour if you slant the facts to fit the desired outcome" (Point
First Writing). This is the most serious failure available in a memo, because
it corrupts a decision, not just a document. When length pressure comes, the
favorable material shrinks first. Cutting the unfavorable material first is
how a memo becomes a brief.

### Provenance discipline

Open the Facts by naming where the facts came from, and state their status in
the first sentence. The default form does this in five words: the facts come
from [source], and "They are reported, not established." Keep that move. Then
maintain the discipline sentence by sentence. Four tiers, descending
reliability (the tiers are an organization of attested practice, not a quoted
taxonomy `[UNVERIFIED]`):

1. **Established of record.** "The court entered judgment on March 3." Cite
   the document.
2. **Documented but not adjudicated.** "The contract, signed April 2,
   provides..." Name the document.
3. **Reported.** "Ms. K. reports that..." "According to the client..." Name the
   reporter.
4. **Alleged.** "The complaint asserts..." "Defendant alleges..." Name the
   alleging party. NWTC's terms: "asserted," "claimed," "apparently,"
   "allegedly."

**Quarantine outside public reporting in its own paragraph**, expressly
flagged as unverified and not to be used until confirmed against a source
document. A newspaper account of what a witness said is two removes from
evidence. The quarantine paragraph is the memo-side application of SKILL.md's
never-invent-a-record-fact rule: a fact you did not verify is not a fact you
may silently launder into the tier above it, and a supervising attorney who
cannot tell your established facts from your reported ones cannot use either.

Facts you do not have get named too, in the Facts and again in the
sensitivity analysis. "If you predict the legal outcome based on an assumption
that does not hold up, your analysis will be seriously flawed" (Point First
Writing).

### Tone

No comment, no application of law, no adjectives doing argument's work. NWTC's
self-check: "Is my bias showing?" "The contract was breached" is a legal
conclusion and belongs in the Discussion; "the guard was hopelessly
inattentive" is advocacy and belongs nowhere.

## 7. Discussion

### The paradigm

IRAC, CREAC, CRAC, and their cousins all describe the same spine: conclusion
or issue first, rule, explanation of the rule from the cases, application to
these facts, conclusion. For an objective memo the best fit is CUNY's CRRACC,
Conclusion, Rule, Rule explanation, Application, **Counterargument**,
Conclusion, because it makes counter-analysis a structural requirement rather
than an optional flourish. Use a separate CRRACC for each issue or sub-issue.
And carry Point First Writing's caution: "No one formula will fit the bill
every time." The formula is scaffolding, not the building.

On a statutory question, the Rule section leads with **the statute's words**,
quoted, then the cases explaining those terms (NWTC). A Wisconsin memo that
leads with cases on a statutory question is structured wrong for its
jurisdiction; Wisconsin statutory construction is text-first.

### The roadmap

Open the Discussion with a paragraph that identifies the issues and governing
rules, restates the short answers, sets out the subparts and how they relate,
and, the item juniors always skip, **names the undisputed issues and says why
they are undisputed**. Two sentences disposing of the three elements nobody
contests saves the reader more time than anything else in the memo, and it
frees the body for the element that is actually fought.

### Thesis headings

Every Discussion heading is a full-sentence proposition, in the default form
bold italic, lettered A through however far the analysis goes. Headings
"announce content and prediction" (Point First Writing): "B. The notice was
timely because service was by mail and § 801.15(5) adds three days" rather
than "B. Timeliness." The headings, read alone, should be the memo's analysis
in miniature. This mirrors the point-heading rule in `argument.md` § 10 with
one genre shift: a memo heading predicts, it does not urge.

### Elements and factors

**Elements** (all required): each gets its own subsection in statutory order.
Dispose of the plainly satisfied ones in the roadmap; spend the memo on the
contested one.

**Factors** (balanced, none dispositive): do not march through eight factors
at equal length in numerical order. Group them: the factors that clearly favor
one side, the factors that clearly favor the other, and the one or two that
will decide it, with a statement of which the court is likely to treat as
controlling. Equal treatment of unequal factors is not neutrality, it is the
absence of prediction. (This elements/factors handling is standard pedagogy
not sourced in the research. `[UNVERIFIED]` as attributed doctrine; correct as
craft.)

### Weave, do not stack

"The busy lawyer wants an analysis, not a book report" (Point First Writing).
After you discuss a case, apply it to your facts immediately, in the same
breath, before the next case. Analogize and distinguish; a summary of five
cases followed by one application paragraph is a book report. Explanatory
parentheticals earn more of their keep in a memo than in a brief, because they
save the supervisor from pulling the case, and they should be factual
("sexual assault joined with an attempt to bribe the same victim") rather than
generic ("holding joinder improper").

### How deep counter-analysis goes

The floor: address the counterarguments that could be raised and show why they
would not prevail (CUNY). Depth is required, "complete and detailed" (NWTC),
where facts are disputed, law is unclear, or the issue is fact-intensive with
no precedent on identical facts. Depth is proportional to the confidence
claimed: the deeper your "probably," the more counter-analysis it owes. And
unlike a brief, a memo states the adverse authority on its own terms first,
fairly, before answering it. If you find yourself introducing every adverse
case with "Although," the memo is arguing.

Case-depth balance: go deep on a case that is central, that must be
distinguished, that is novel, or where the law is evolving away from its
reasoning. Go shallow on a case that merely illustrates a settled point,
that is a well-known leading case, or that carries little weight.

## 8. When there is no controlling Wisconsin authority

Never write only "no Wisconsin case has decided this." That sentence begins
the analysis; it does not end it. The honest sequence:

**First, say precisely what you searched and did not find, and scope the
claim.** These are different findings and the memo must say which one it makes:
no Wisconsin appellate case has addressed the question at all; no Wisconsin
case has addressed these facts under a rule Wisconsin has otherwise adopted;
Wisconsin authority exists but is unpublished; Wisconsin authority exists but
is arguably distinguishable. State the search scope: which databases, which
date range, which terms. The supervising attorney needs to know whether "no
case" means no case, or no case found in two hours. On unpublished opinions,
citation is governed by Wis. Stat. § 809.23(3) `[UNVERIFIED]`; the research
did not retrieve its text. Read it before citing or relying on an unpublished
decision, and note that authored opinions issued on or after July 1, 2009 are
treated differently from earlier ones.

**Second, establish the framework Wisconsin would start from.** There is
nearly always a Wisconsin statute whose text governs, a Wisconsin case
stating the rule at a higher level of generality, a Wisconsin case adopting
the relevant Restatement section or uniform act, or a Wisconsin decision in an
analogous doctrinal area.

**Third, go to persuasive authority, and rank it.** A defensible ordering
(this ranking is a synthesis, `[UNVERIFIED]` as a quoted hierarchy): Wisconsin
federal courts construing Wisconsin law; decisions of states whose statute
Wisconsin copied or that share the same uniform act; Restatements and
commentary Wisconsin has cited elsewhere; the state-court split, characterized
honestly by count and by trend; Wisconsin legislative history and Judicial
Council Committee Notes; treatises and law reviews.

**Fourth, predict anyway, and say what the prediction rests on.** The model
sentence ends: "This is a prediction from analogous authority, not an
application of controlling law, and it carries correspondingly less
confidence." That sentence is the honest formulation. Use it or its
equivalent every time.

**Fifth, flag the strategic consequence.** First impression means an argument
the other side has not seen either, a higher chance of appellate review, and a
higher chance the trial court gets it wrong in either direction. Say so.

## 9. Sensitivity analysis

Give this its own numbered section, between the Discussion and the
Conclusion. No writing-center source names it as a genre requirement
`[UNVERIFIED]` as a convention, but every component is attested, and the
argument for promoting it to a section is practical: the supervising
attorney's next action is usually driven by this material, not by the Brief
Answers, and material that drives action should not be scattered through the
Discussion or buried in a footnote. A memo that states its verdicts without
stating what would flip them is a photograph with no depth of field.

Four things belong here, separated because they call for different follow-up:

**(a) Load-bearing facts.** The assumptions the prediction rests on. For each:
the fact, its source and provenance tier, and what happens if it is wrong.
"This analysis assumes the notice was mailed March 3. That date comes from the
client's calendar entry, not a postmark. If the actual date was March 5 or
later, the answer flips from probably yes to no." The admission test: would
changing this fact change a Brief Answer? If yes, it goes on the list. Be
especially explicit where a conclusion is load-bearing on a **single
unverified fact**; that is the exact situation the quarantine paragraph in the
Facts exists to surface, and this section is where its consequence is priced.

**(b) Open facts.** What is not yet known, why it matters, and, the part that
makes the section useful, **what would establish it**: the signature page, the
metadata on the emailed PDF, the certified mail receipt, the deposition of the
recipient. This converts a list of gaps into a work plan.

**(c) Missing documents.** By name, who has them, and what discovery device
would get them. Try to obtain missing facts before drafting; this section is
the residue after that effort, not a substitute for it.

**(d) Legal instability.** What could change even if the facts do not: pending
supreme court review, a recent court of appeals decision not yet final, a
statutory amendment with an effective date, a split in the federal courts.

## 10. Conclusion and recommendations

The Conclusion opens with an assessment paragraph, the overall read in prose,
then numbered next steps, each led by a bolded action label. Urgency tags on
the labels, "(immediate)," "(now)," "(before the scheduling conference),"
are the default form and they work: they convert a reading into a to-do list.

Recommendations are permitted in a memo to a supervising attorney; Georgetown
expressly contemplates a Conclusion that "recommends strategy with reasoning."
Two constraints. Keep recommendations visibly separate from prediction, so the
reader can tell where the analysis stops and the judgment call begins. And
frame every recommendation as a next step for the attorney, never as advice to
the client: "Recommended: (1) obtain the certified mail receipt; (2) if it
shows March 5, re-run the § ____ analysis" is safe. "The client should file
suit before March 15" is not language a nonlawyer's memo may carry. See § 12.

The default form closes with a short first-person note saying candidly what the
writer hoped to find and did not. Keep it. It is the single most trust-
building paragraph in the genre, because it proves the writer looked for the
answer they wanted and reported the one they got.

## 11. Work product and privilege

### The statute

Wis. Stat. § 804.01(2)(c)1 (quoted from an unofficial mirror; read the
official text before relying on it):

> "Subject to par. (d) a party may obtain discovery of documents and tangible
> things otherwise discoverable under par. (a) and prepared in anticipation of
> litigation or for trial by or for another party or by or for that other
> party's representative (including an attorney, consultant, surety,
> indemnitor, insurer, or agent) only upon a showing that the party seeking
> discovery has substantial need of the materials in the preparation of the
> case and that the party seeking discovery is unable without undue hardship
> to obtain the substantial equivalent of the materials by other means. In
> ordering discovery of such materials when the required showing has been
> made, the court shall protect against disclosure of the mental impressions,
> conclusions, opinions, or legal theories of an attorney or other
> representative of a party concerning the litigation. This protection is
> forfeited as to any material disclosed inadvertently in circumstances in
> which, if the material were a lawyer-client communication, the disclosure
> would constitute a forfeiture under s. 905.03 (5). This protection is waived
> as to any material disclosed by the party or the party's representative if
> the disclosure is not inadvertent."

Four operative points. The protection is qualified, not absolute: ordinary
work product yields on substantial need plus inability without undue hardship.
Opinion work product gets more: even on that showing, the court "shall protect
against disclosure of the mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal
theories of an attorney **or other representative** of a party," and "or other
representative" is the phrase that carries a paralegal's analysis into the
protected category. The trigger is "prepared in anticipation of litigation or
for trial," which is the condition most often missed; see below. And waiver is
sharp-edged: non-inadvertent disclosure by the party or its representative
waives outright, while inadvertent disclosure forfeits only under the
§ 905.03(5) standard.

A nonlawyer's memo qualifies. The Wisconsin Court of Appeals in *Borgwardt v.
Redlin*, No. 94-2701 (Wis. Ct. App. Aug. 8, 1995) (published): "The
work-product-doctrine qualified privilege protects from disclosure those
investigations and analyses made by a party or by the party's agent, who may
but need not be a lawyer." `[UNVERIFIED]` as to the reporter citation: the
opinion is marked published but the research could not confirm a Wis. 2d or
N.W.2d cite; look it up before citing *Borgwardt* in anything delivered. The
foundational case is *State ex rel. Dudek v. Circuit Court for Milwaukee
County*, 34 Wis. 2d 559, 150 N.W.2d 387 (1967) `[UNVERIFIED]`; the citation
was corroborated by secondary sources but the opinion was not read. Do not
quote *Dudek* without reading it.

The lawyer-client privilege, Wis. Stat. § 905.03, runs alongside and covers
different ground. A "representative of the lawyer" is "one employed to assist
the lawyer in the rendition of professional legal services," § 905.03(1)(c),
so a paralegal is inside the privilege for confidential communications. But
privilege protects communications; work product protects materials. A memo of
pure legal analysis with no client communication in it is protected, if at
all, by work product only.

### The condition that actually matters

"Prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial" is a limit, not a
formality. A general research memo on an abstract question, no client, no
pending or reasonably anticipated dispute, is not trial-preparation material
and the legend will not make it so. For the memo to be work product it should
be prepared for an identified client matter in which litigation is pending or
reasonably anticipated, at the direction of the supervising attorney, and
address that matter's actual questions.

### What the legend does and does not do

The legend does not make an unprivileged document privileged: "how you label
is not dispositive," and content controls. It does not protect a memo prepared
outside anticipated litigation, and it does not prevent waiver by deliberate
disclosure. What it does: it evidences intent and reasonable precautions,
which bears directly on the § 905.03(5) inadvertent-disclosure analysis; it
warns everyone downstream not to forward, produce, or attach the document;
and it supports a clawback demand and a privilege-log entry. Mark case by
case, not by blanket footer; put the legend at the top of page 1 above the To
line, and repeat it in the footer of every page when appropriate. The following
example assumes both the asserted protection and counsel's role have been
confirmed; otherwise omit or narrow the label:

```
PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL
ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT · PREPARED IN ANTICIPATION OF LITIGATION
Prepared at the direction of counsel. Wis. Stat. § 804.01(2)(c)1.
Do not forward, produce, or disclose without the express direction
of the supervising attorney.
```

Include "prepared at the direction of counsel" only when it is true. Do not infer
privilege, anticipated litigation, an attorney-client relationship, or lawyer
review merely because the task is called a legal memorandum.

### The pro se variant

When the user is self-represented, the doctrine follows the party,
not the lawyer: § 804.01(2)(c)1 protects materials prepared "by or for
another party," and *Borgwardt* says the preparer "may but need not be a
lawyer." A pro se litigant's own trial-preparation materials can be work
product. Work prepared independently without an attorney-client communication
does not become attorney-client privileged by being labeled so. If limited-scope
counsel or legal consultations are involved, assess those actual communications
separately under § 905.03. For qualifying materials prepared by the party alone,
the example legend drops the word "attorney":

```
CONFIDENTIAL · TRIAL PREPARATION MATERIAL
Wis. Stat. § 804.01(2)(c)1.
```

## 12. The UPL boundary

This section addresses a nonlawyer's work for a supervising attorney. Determine
the actual writer, audience, supervision, and applicable authority; phrasing a
document as a prediction is not itself an exemption from practice rules.

### The rules

SCR 23.01 defines the practice of law as "the application of legal principles
and judgment with regard to the circumstances or objectives of another entity
or person(s) where there is a client relationship of trust or reliance and
which require the knowledge, judgment, and skill of a person trained as a
lawyer," and its first enumerated category is "[g]iving advice or counsel to
others as to their legal rights or the legal rights or responsibilities of
others for fees or other consideration." SCR 23.02 confines practice to
licensed active State Bar members, and Wis. Stat. § 757.30 is the criminal
backstop: practicing law without a license is punishable by fine or up to a
year in the county jail, and § 757.30(2) reaches anyone who "for compensation
or pecuniary reward gives professional legal advice not incidental to his or
her usual or ordinary business." Enforcement is reportedly weak; do not treat
that as permission, because the professional-responsibility consequences land
on the supervising attorney regardless.

The safe harbor is SCR 23.02(2)(m): "Acting as a nonlawyer assistant under
the supervision of a lawyer in compliance with SCR 20:5.3." Note the
condition. The exception exists only where actual supervision exists. Under
SCR 20:5.3(b), a lawyer with direct supervisory authority over a nonlawyer
"shall make reasonable efforts to ensure that the person's conduct is
compatible with the professional obligations of the lawyer," and under
20:5.3(c) the lawyer answers for conduct the lawyer orders, ratifies, or
knowingly fails to remediate. Wisconsin does not license paralegals;
certification is voluntary; but all paralegals must be attorney-supervised
and cannot practice independently (WisBar).

### What this means for a memo

The ABA Model Guidelines (persuasive in Wisconsin, not adopted by rule, and
read via a secondary reproduction, `[UNVERIFIED]` as to exact wording) permit
delegating "factual investigation and research, legal research, and the
preparation of legal documents" under supervision, and forbid delegating
responsibility for a legal opinion rendered to a client.

**The paralegal may:** conduct the research; read and analyze the statutes,
rules, and cases; synthesize the authority; draft the entire memo, Discussion
and predicted outcome included; cite-check; identify open facts and missing
documents; recommend next steps to the attorney.

**The supervising attorney must:** assign with adequate instruction, monitor,
and review the completed memo before anyone acts on it. The lawyer, not the
paralegal, is responsible for the work product and for any advice that
reaches the client.

**For an internal memo prepared by a nonlawyer:** direct the draft to the
supervising attorney and obtain that lawyer's substantive review before it is
used for client advice. Do not independently render a legal opinion to the client.
The distinction rests on the actual work, relationship, supervision, and rules,
not on replacing "you should" with "a court would likely conclude."

Primary sources: [SCR ch. 23](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/scrule/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=692090)
and [SCR 20:5.3](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/scrule/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=1121033).
Check their current versions and applicable exceptions for the actual role.

### The language the memo carries

Three things. The From line states the nonlawyer title plainly: "Legal
Assistant" or "Paralegal," never a title that reads as a lawyer's. The
signature block matches. And adjacent to the work product legend, a
review-status legend. No Wisconsin authority prescribes specific wording
`[UNVERIFIED]`; this form is constructed from SCR 20:5.3, SCR 23.02(2)(m),
and the ABA Guidelines, and the supervising attorney should approve the office's
version:

```
This memorandum was prepared by a nonlawyer at the direction of and for
the review of the supervising attorney. It is an internal research and
analysis document, not legal advice and not a legal opinion, and it may
not be transmitted to the client or relied on by anyone until reviewed
and approved by the supervising attorney. See SCR 20:5.3; SCR 23.02(2)(m).
```

That is the whole discipline. Address the memo up, not out; predict, do not
counsel; label the writer honestly; and let the attorney's review be the step
that turns analysis into advice.

## 13. Citation in a memo

SCR 80.02 by its terms governs documents filed with the appellate courts, not
office memos. Use it anyway. A memo is often the first draft of a brief, and a
memo already in filing form lifts into one without re-citing. So: public
domain citation where it exists, all three forms on first reference, paragraph
pincites for post-1999 decisions, case names italicized, and short forms kept
internally consistent, exactly as `citation.md` prescribes.

What legitimately changes in a memo: pincites matter more, not less, because
the supervisor will re-read the sources against the memo. Explanatory
parentheticals earn more of their keep. In-text attribution is acceptable
where a brief would use a formal citation sentence. And signals are load-
bearing information, not rhetorical concessions: *but see* and *contra* in a
memo are the adverse-authority disclosure duty doing its work. What does not
relax: verification. Every cite in a memo gets the same adversarial check as
a cite in a filing, because the memo's reader will act on it without checking.

## 14. Length, and how to cut

No source fixes a length, and Bloomberg Law's real answer is the right one:
read the office's prior memos, because length is an office norm. Working ranges,
practitioner convention only `[UNVERIFIED]`: a single narrow question on
settled law, one to three pages; a single question with a contested
application, four to eight; multi-issue or first impression, ten to twenty;
past twenty, add a table of contents and ask whether it should be two memos.

The governing principle: length is a cost. The memo exists to save the
attorney time, and a memo that takes longer to read than the research took to
do has failed regardless of quality.

Six cuts, in order of how much they usually remove:

1. Cut every fact whose change would not change an answer, unless it is
   needed for context.
2. Cut case discussion that does no work here: cases that illustrate a point
   already made, well-known leading cases needing only a cite, low-weight
   authority.
3. Collapse the uncontested elements into the roadmap. Two sentences.
4. Cut string cites to the one or two best cases, with a *see also* only if
   truly needed.
5. Cut the second telling. Roadmap, section, and Conclusion each state the
   point once at their own altitude, not three times at the same one.
6. Cut the research narrative. "I first searched Westlaw for..." is process,
   not product. The exception is § 8: where you found nothing, the search
   scope is substantive and stays.

What never gets cut, under any pressure: adverse authority, unfavorable
facts, the counter-analysis on the contested issue, and the sensitivity
analysis. When the memo must shrink, shrink the favorable material first.
That inversion is the discipline that separates a memo from a brief.

## 15. Failure modes in memos by junior writers

By section, with the tell for each.

**Questions Presented.** Assumes the answer ("is the plainly untimely notice
barred"). Contains no facts, so it is a topic, not a question. Names no
jurisdiction. Runs in a different order than the Discussion.

**Brief Answers.** Buries the verdict in the third sentence or omits it. Not
self-contained; refers to facts the reader has not read yet. Runs a page.
Hedges without saying why: "it depends on the facts," with no statement of
which facts.

**Facts.** Omits the bad facts, the most serious failure in the genre. Argues
through adjectives. States legal conclusions as facts. Contains facts the
Discussion never uses, or the Discussion uses facts the section never states.
Never distinguishes alleged from established.

**Discussion.** The book report: cases summarized in sequence, one application
paragraph at the end. Rule quoted, then applied, with no explanation of how
courts have actually used it in between. String cites for the uncontroversial
proposition, nothing for the contested one. Counter-analysis as a one-sentence
dismissal. Topic headings instead of thesis headings. A statute cited but its
words never quoted, which in Wisconsin is a structural error, not a style one.
No roadmap, so the reader discovers the structure by finishing the memo.

**Prediction.** Advocacy creep, § 1. Overclaiming: "Yes" where "probably yes"
is honest. Underclaiming: hedging everything until the memo has no content; a
memo that concludes "the outcome is uncertain" without saying which way it
leans has not been written. Reporting the research instead of answering the
question. Not saying what was not found.

**Process.** Cites not verified against the source, the single most damaging
error and the one AI-assisted drafting makes most likely; every case name,
pincite, and quotation gets opened and confirmed, which is what the
conventions block promises. Currency not checked. Answering a different
question than the one assigned; if the assigned question turns out to be the
wrong question, say so and answer the assigned one anyway. No date, or a
stale one.

## 16. Table of contents, numbered fact paragraphs, appendix

No source prescribes rules for any of these; what follows is practitioner
convention `[UNVERIFIED]`.

**Table of contents.** Add one past roughly fifteen to twenty pages, or at
three or more independent issues, or when the memo will circulate to several
readers or be filed for reuse. Omit under ten pages; the thesis headings
already are one. If included, generate it from the thesis headings, so the
contents page reads as a summary of the analysis rather than a list of
topics. That is the strongest argument for thesis headings in the first
place.

**Numbered fact paragraphs.** Use them when the Discussion will cross-
reference facts repeatedly ("as set out in ¶ 7"), when the facts are long or
contested, when the attorney will annotate and return the memo, or when the
memo will become the factual basis for an affidavit or a summary judgment
submission. The Wisconsin payoff is real: numbered memo facts convert cleanly
into the numbered paragraphs of a § 887.015 declaration or the proposed
findings Trempealeau Rule 6 requires. Skip the numbering for a short
narrative; numbering a five-paragraph chronology adds friction and nothing
else.

**Appendix.** For the full text of a long statute the memo construes, a
timeline that would break the Facts narrative, a chart of the leading cases,
or the out-of-state authority table from a § 8 first-impression memo. Never
for analysis: if the reader must open the appendix to understand the
prediction, the material belongs in the body. Never a full case; pincite it.

**And one line that beats all three:** a file, date, and currency line at the
top. "Prepared August 26, 2026. Law current through [edition and Act]. File
____." In a practice where memos get reused, that line does more good than a
table of contents ever will, and the conventions block in § 3 already carries
most of it.
